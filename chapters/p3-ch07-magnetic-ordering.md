## 7.1 Introduction

Our discussion of magnetism up to this point has been concerned with weak magnetic interactions, corresponding to energies of less than 1 meV for magnetic fields of $\sim$10 tesla. We shall now consider strong magnetic interactions where the magnetic moments on neighboring atomic sites interact with each other collectively and produce a magnetic ordering in the crystal. Various magnetic orderings are possible:

$$\begin{array}{ccc}
\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow\uparrow & \text{ferromagnet} \\[4pt]
\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow\downarrow\uparrow & \text{antiferromagnet}
\end{array}$$

**ferromagnet** -- the magnetic moments (spins) all line up parallel to one another, and

**antiferromagnet** -- equal magnetic moments (spins) on nearest neighbor sites which tend to line up antiparallel.

More complicated arrangements are possible like a

$$\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow\downarrow\Uparrow \quad \text{ferrimagnet}$$

which has a basic antiparallel arrangement of the magnetic moments (spins) but the magnitudes of the moments in the two directions are unequal, giving rise to a net magnetization.

Various spiral arrangements of the moments can occur and canted arrangements of the moments are also common in real materials. These various forms of magnetism will be discussed in the next few lectures.

All these forms of magnetism have the following common features:

- At high temperatures they are all paramagnetic
- Below an ordering temperature $T_c$, they all have ordered magnetic moments (spins).

We shall discuss the ferromagnetic ordering first because it is simplest, though similar arguments can be made for the other magnetically ordered systems. We will start in the paramagnetic regime with which we already have some familiarity.

## 7.2 The Weiss Molecular Field Model

In the magnetically ordered regime, we say that under the action of an external magnetic field $\vec{H}$, the total magnetization that is achieved depends not only on this applied field but on a molecular field $\vec{H}_M$ generated by the magnetization of the medium itself (see {numref}`fig-p3-ch07-1`). $\vec{H}_{\rm total}$ is called the mean field:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-1
\vec{H}_{\rm total} = (\vec{H} + \vec{H}_M).
```

The molecular field $\vec{H}_M$ is assumed to be proportional to the magnetization $M$:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-2
\vec{H}_M = \lambda_M \vec{M},
```

and can be considered to arise from the other dipole moments in the medium (see {numref}`fig-p3-ch07-1`). In the usual derivation of the Curie law (see 3.4), we assume that the magnetic field due to the other paramagnetic ions is small compared with the external magnetic field and we ignore $\vec{H}_M$ completely. For ferromagnetic systems $\vec{H}_M$ is very large, in fact much larger than the magnetic fields that are achieved in the laboratory.

For a strongly magnetic material in the paramagnetic region, the total magnetic field $\vec{H}_{\rm total} = (\vec{H} + \vec{H}_M)$ tends to line up the magnetic moments along $\vec{H}_{\rm total}$, while the thermal energy tends to randomize the motion. The derivation given for the magnetization (see Eq. 3.52) still holds except that the magnetic induction $\vec{B}$ includes the effect of both the applied field and the molecular field. From Eq. 3.52

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-3
M = Ng\mu_B j\,B_j(x); \qquad x = \frac{g\mu_B j B}{k_B T}
```

where $N$ is the atomic density and we now write

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-4
x = \frac{g\mu_B j\hat{\mu}(H + H_M)}{k_B T}; \quad B = \hat{\mu}(H + H_M).
```

At high temperatures ($x\ll 1$), the susceptibility will be shown to follow the Curie--Weiss law. In this limit,

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-5
B_j(x)\sim x\left(\frac{j+1}{3j}\right)
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-6
M\simeq \frac{Ng^2\mu_B^2(j+1)j\hat{\mu}}{3k_BT}(H+H_M)
= \left(\frac{C}{T}\right)(H+H_M)
```

where the Curie constant is:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-7
C = \frac{Ng^2\mu_B^2 j(j+1)\hat{\mu}}{3k_B}.
```

Writing $H_M=\lambda_M M$, we obtain

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-8
M\left[1-\frac{C\lambda_M}{T}\right] = \left(\frac{C}{T}\right)H
```

But $M=\chi H$ so that

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-9
\chi=\frac{C}{T}\frac{1}{(1-C\lambda_M/T)} = \frac{C}{T-C\lambda_M}
```

and we obtain the Curie--Weiss Law

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-10
\chi = \frac{C}{T-T_c}
```

which is valid for $T\gg T_c$ and in which the constant $C\lambda_M$, which is independent of $T$, defines the Curie temperature

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-11
T_c = C\lambda_M.
```

The Curie--Weiss law is valid only in the high temperature limit and is well satisfied for the common ferromagnetic materials in that limit. As an example, consider iron for which the ordering temperature is 1043K or 770$^\circ$C. The paramagnetic region occurs for $T$ greater than the magnetic ordering temperatures and the Curie--Weiss law holds for temperatures much larger than 770$^\circ$C.

## 7.3 The Spontaneous Magnetization

According to the molecular field model for a ferromagnet, the expression for the magnetization $M$ in the paramagnetic region is (see Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-3`):

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-12
M = Ng\mu_B j\,B_j(x),
```

where the argument $x$ of the Brillouin function is

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-13
x = \frac{g\mu_B j\hat{\mu}(H+\lambda_MM)}{k_BT},
```

in which $H_M=\lambda_M M$ is written for the molecular field. Clearly this equation has a solution $M=0$ for $H=0$ and this solution exists for all temperatures which says that there is no net magnetization unless there is an applied magnetic field.

However, in ferromagnetic materials, a molecular field $H_M=\lambda_M M$ is present and a solution of this equation is possible even in zero magnetic field, provided that $\lambda_M M$ is large enough compared with $k_B T$. We will now see how to determine the value of $M$ for which spontaneous magnetization occurs. That is, with no external field present, a small fluctuation in the local magnetization causes the neighboring moments to align, yielding further magnetization until an ordered state is formed and in this sense, the disordered state is unstable.

To find the spontaneous magnetization we set $H=0$ in the Brillouin function which enters Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-12`

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-14
M = Ng\mu_B j\,B_j(x')
```

where $x'$ denotes the variable $x$ when $H=0$:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-15
x' = \frac{g\mu_B j\hat{\mu}\lambda_M M}{k_B T}.
```

The simplest method to find a solution to Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-14` is the graphical method where we plot $Ng\mu_B j\,B_j(x')\equiv f(M)$ as a function of $M$. The solution to Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-14` is the intersection point in {numref}`fig-p3-ch07-2` which defines $M_S$, the spontaneous magnetization, and $M_S$ is present with no applied magnetic field.

As the temperature increases, the initial slope of the right hand side of the equation decreases and $M_S(T)$ decreases and eventually goes to zero at a critical temperature $T'_c$. For $T=T'_c$ the initial slope of $f(M)$ is exactly unity.

Values for the spontaneous magnetization allow us to calculate the magnitude of the molecular field. Let us take the values in Table 7.1 for Fe and take $g=2$, $j=1$. For 3d transition metals, the orbital moment is quenched. From Table 7.1 we obtain $n_B=gj=2.2$ for Fe. By setting the slope $(\partial f(M)/\partial M)=1$, we define $T'_c$ from Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-6` and solve for $\lambda_M$ and make use of the relation $M_s=N gj\mu_B$,

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-18
\lambda_M = \frac{3k_BT_c}{N g^2 \mu_B^2 j(j+1)\hat{\mu}} = \frac{3k_BT_c}{M_s g \mu_B (j+1)\hat{\mu}},
```

which for Fe becomes $\lambda_M\sim 5000$. With $H_M\sim\lambda_M M_S$ ($M_S(0)=1740$ gauss), we therefore obtain $H_M\sim 10^7$ gauss for the internal field in iron. This magnitude is much larger than any applied field we can generate in the laboratory.

Let us think about that large magnitude of $H_M$ more closely. The old picture of the Weiss molecular field (see 7.2) in terms of magnetic moments would give a dipole field of $\mu_B/a^3$ which might be as much as $10^4$ gauss at the position of the nearest magnetic moment, but hardly more than that. To account for this strong magnetic field we need an electrostatic interaction of much larger magnitude than the magnetic dipole-dipole interaction.

Such a microscopic model is provided by the Heisenberg Hamiltonian which was proposed about 25 years ago after the introduction of the molecular field theory by Weiss on the basis of quantum mechanics.

## 7.4 The Exchange Interaction

Ferromagnetism is a cooperative phenomenon -- an isolated electron or ion cannot become ferromagnetic. To achieve the high internal fields required by the molecular field theory, an electrostatic interaction must be introduced. Such an interaction arises through so-called **exchange effects** which are associated with the Pauli principle and with the spin of the electron.

To understand the origin of the exchange interaction, consider the Hamiltonian for a 2-electron system

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-19
\mathcal{H} = \frac{p_1^2}{2m} + \frac{p_2^2}{2m}
- \frac{Ze^2}{r_1} - \frac{Ze^2}{r_2}
+ \frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}
= \mathcal{H}_1 + \mathcal{H}_2 + V_{12}
```

where

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-20
\mathcal{H}_1 = \frac{p_1^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r_1}
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-21
\mathcal{H}_2 = \frac{p_2^2}{2m} - \frac{Ze^2}{r_2}
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-22
V_{12} = \frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_1 - \vec{r}_2|}.
```

The one-electron Hamiltonians $\mathcal{H}_1(\vec{r}_1)$ and $\mathcal{H}_2(\vec{r}_2)$ can be solved directly each yielding an energy eigenvalue $E_0$, the energy for an electron in the field of a nucleus of charge $Z$. On the other hand, the interaction term $V_{12}$ expresses the Coulomb repulsion between the two electrons. The Coulomb energy is found approximately by perturbation theory using the eigenfunctions of $\mathcal{H}_1$ and $\mathcal{H}_2$ which are written as $\psi_1(\vec{r}_1)$ and $\psi_2(\vec{r}_2)$. We thus write the Coulomb energy

$$C_{12} = \int\psi_1^*(\vec{r}_1)\psi_2^*(\vec{r}_2)
\frac{e^2}{|\vec{r}_1-\vec{r}_2|}
\psi_1(\vec{r}_1)\psi_2(\vec{r}_2)\,d^3r_1 d^3r_2. \tag{7.23}$$

In writing down the states for a 2-electron system, we recognize that the two electrons are identical and indistinguishable. We are also required to satisfy the Pauli Exclusion Principle which states that the 2-electron wave function must be totally antisymmetric under the interchange of the 2 electrons. We normally write the total wave function as a product of a spatial wave function with a spin function. In this situation, we have two options:

1. the spin function is symmetric and the spatial function is antisymmetric, or
2. the spin function is antisymmetric and the spatial function is symmetric.

The symmetric two electron spin function can be made in one of three ways:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-24
\chi^S(1,1)=\alpha(1)\,\alpha(2)
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-25
\chi^S(1,-1)=\beta(1)\,\beta(2)
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-26
\chi^S(1,0)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\alpha(1)\beta(2)+\alpha(2)\beta(1)]
```

where $\alpha$ denotes the spin up function and $\beta$ the spin down function. Thus the symmetric spin function is a **triplet state** which goes with the antisymmetric spatial wave function

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-27
\psi_{12}^A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\psi_1(1)\psi_2(2)-\psi_1(2)\psi_2(1)].
```

On the other hand, the antisymmetric spin function can only be constituted in one way

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-28
\chi_{12}^A = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\alpha(1)\beta(2)-\alpha(2)\beta(1)]
```

and this forms a product with the symmetric spatial function

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-29
\psi_{12}^S = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}[\psi_1(1)\psi_2(2)+\psi_1(2)\psi_2(1)].
```

Although the Hamiltonian in Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-19` does not contain any explicit dependence on the electron spin, the energy of the system will be different if we calculate the expectation value for the Coulomb repulsion energy $V_{12}$ in the symmetric or antisymmetric spatial state. Explicitly this difference arises from

$$\tfrac{1}{2}\int[\psi_1^*(1)\psi_2^*(2)\pm\psi_1^*(2)\psi_2^*(1)]V_{12}[\psi_1(1)\psi_2(2)\pm\psi_1(2)\psi_2(1)]\,d^3r_1 d^3r_2 = C_{12}\pm J_{12}, \tag{7.30}$$

where $J_{12}$ is the so-called **exchange energy**:

$$J_{12} \equiv \int\psi_1^*(1)\psi_2^*(2)[e^2/|\vec{r}_1-\vec{r}_2|]\,\psi_1(2)\psi_2(1)\,d^3r_1 d^3r_2 \tag{7.31}$$

and $J_{12}=J_{21}$. If $J_{12}>0$, the triplet state lies lower. Here the spins are lined up and $S=1$. For the singlet state we have $S=0$. We then can write:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-32
\vec{S} = \vec{S}_1 + \vec{S}_2
```

so that

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-33
S^2=(\vec{S}_1+\vec{S}_2)\cdot(\vec{S}_1+\vec{S}_2)=S_1^2+S_2^2+2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2.
```

For the triplet state, Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-33` yields

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-34
2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2=s(s+1)-s_1(s_1+1)-s_2(s_2+1)=2-(3/4)-(3/4)=1,
```

and for the singlet state

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-35
2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2=0-(3/4)-(3/4)=-(3/2).
```

Therefore we can write

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-36
\tfrac{1}{2}+2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2=1
```

for the spin symmetric (triplet) state, and

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-37
\tfrac{1}{2}+2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2=-1
```

for the spin antisymmetric (singlet) state, which allows us to write

$$\Delta E=C_{12}-\tfrac{1}{2}J_{12}-2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2 J_{12}. \tag{7.38}$$

The term $[-2\vec{S}_1\cdot\vec{S}_2]J_{12}$ is called the **Heisenberg Hamiltonian**.

To see how to generalize the exchange interaction to more than one electron we write the wave function in the form of a determinant (called the Slater determinant). For $n$ electrons the Slater determinant is written as

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-39
\Psi(\vec{x}_1,\ldots,\vec{x}_n) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}
\begin{vmatrix}
\psi_1(\vec{x}_1) & \psi_1(\vec{x}_2) & \cdots & \psi_1(\vec{x}_n) \\
\psi_2(\vec{x}_1) & \psi_2(\vec{x}_2) & \cdots & \psi_2(\vec{x}_n) \\
\vdots & \vdots & & \vdots \\
\psi_n(\vec{x}_1) & \psi_n(\vec{x}_2) & \cdots & \psi_n(\vec{x}_n)
\end{vmatrix},
```

and this determinantal form guarantees that no two electrons are in the same set of quantum states.

The Heisenberg Hamiltonian is written as

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-47
\mathcal{H}=-2\sum'_{i,j}J_{ij}\,\vec{S}_i\cdot\vec{S}_j
```

where the sum is over pairs of electrons. Considering only nearest neighbor interactions with $z$ identical neighbors:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-48
\mathcal{H}=-2zJ_{ij}\,\vec{S}_i\cdot\vec{S}_j.
```

We now show how the Heisenberg Hamiltonian gives rise to a relation for the mean or molecular field. The spin $\vec{S}_j$ can be identified with a magnetic moment $\vec{\mu}_j$

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-49
\vec{\mu}_j=g\mu_B\,\vec{S}_j.
```

This magnetic moment sees a field $\vec{B}_j=2\sum'_i J_{ij}\vec{S}_i/g\mu_B$. If all nearest neighbors are identical, then

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-50
\vec{B}_j=\frac{2zJ_{ij}\,\vec{S}_i}{g\mu_B}.
```

We identify this with the molecular field. The spontaneous magnetization at $T=0$ is

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-51
\vec{M}_S=N\vec{\mu}_j=Ng\mu_B\,\vec{S}_j
```

Thus the molecular field can be written as

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-52
\hat{\mu}\lambda_M\vec{M}_S=\vec{B}=\frac{2zJ_{ij}\,\vec{S}_i}{g\mu_B}
=\frac{2zJ_{ij}\,\vec{M}_S}{g\mu_B Ng\mu_B},
```

so that

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-53
\vec{B}=\frac{2zJ_{ij}\,\vec{M}_S}{N g^2\mu_B^2}.
```

From Eq. {eq}`eq-p3-ch07-52` we obtain

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-54
\lambda_M=\frac{2zJ_{ij}}{Ng^2\mu_B^2\hat{\mu}}.
```

Since the molecular field theory gives an independent value of $\lambda_M$, the exchange integral $J_{ij}$ can be estimated from the measured Curie temperature:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-55
\lambda_M=\frac{T_c}{C}=\frac{3k_BT_c}{Ng^2\mu_B^2 j(j+1)\hat{\mu}}
```

so that

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-56
J_{ij}=\frac{Ng^2\mu_B^2\lambda_M\hat{\mu}}{2z}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{k_BT_c}{z j(j+1)}.
```

For iron, $z=8$, $T_c=1043$ K, $j=1$: $J_{ij}\sim 0.008$ eV.

In carrying out practical calculations involving the Heisenberg Hamiltonian, the Ising model approximation is often introduced:

$$\mathcal{H}=-2\sum'_{i,j}J_{ij}(S_x^i S_x^j+S_y^i S_y^j+S_z^i S_z^j) \tag{7.59}$$

According to the Ising model, only the $z$ component survives:

$$\mathcal{H}=-2\sum'_{i,j}J_{ij}S_z^i S_z^j \tag{7.60}$$

## 7.5 Antiferromagnetism and Ferrimagnetism

In a ferromagnet, the exchange energy is positive $J>0$ so that the ground state is an ordered state with all spins lined up. In an antiferromagnet, $J<0$, the spins are lined up antiparallel in the ordered state as shown in {numref}`fig-p3-ch07-5` for MnO.

To describe an antiferromagnet, introduce two sublattices: an A sublattice for spin-$\uparrow$ sites and a B sublattice for spin-$\downarrow$ sites. For a ferrimagnet (e.g., magnetite Fe$_3$O$_4$ crystallizing in the spinel structure), different exchange integrals $J_{AA}$, $J_{AB}$, $J_{BB}$ govern interactions depending on sublattices. This is represented by molecular fields:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-61
\vec{H}_A=-\lambda_{MA}\vec{M}_A-\lambda_{MAB}\vec{M}_B,
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-62
\vec{H}_B=-\lambda_{MBA}\vec{M}_A-\lambda_{MB}\vec{M}_B.
```

For a simple antiferromagnet considering only nearest neighbor interactions:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-63
\vec{H}_A=-\lambda_{MAB}\vec{M}_B,\quad \vec{H}_B=-\lambda_{MAB}\vec{M}_A.
```

Applying the analysis of 7.2 to each sublattice independently yields coupled linear equations whose non-trivial solution condition gives the ferrimagnetic Curie temperature:

$$\begin{vmatrix} T_c+C\lambda_{MA} & \lambda_{MAB}C_A \\ \lambda_{MAB}C_B & T_c+\lambda_{MB}C_B \end{vmatrix}=0. \tag{7.67}$$

For the antiferromagnetic case ($\lambda_{MA}=0$, $\lambda_{MB}=0$, $C_A=C_B=C$):

$$\begin{vmatrix} T_c & \lambda_{MAB}C \\ \lambda_{MAB}C & T_c \end{vmatrix}=0 \tag{7.69}$$

or $T_c=\lambda_{MAB}C$. Writing $\lambda_{MAB}=\lambda_M$:

$$M_B=\frac{CH}{T+\lambda_MC} \tag{7.75}$$

yielding

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-78
M=M_A+M_B=\frac{2CH}{T+T_c},
```

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-79
\chi=\frac{2C}{T+T_c}.
```

:align: center
Fig. 7.6: Temperature dependence of the magnetic susceptibility in paramagnets, ferromagnets, and antiferromagnets. Below the Neel temperature of an antiferromagnet, the spins have antiparallel orientations; the susceptibility attains its maximum value at $T_N$.

In the perpendicular orientation ($\vec{H}\perp$ sublattice magnetization):

$$\chi_\perp\rightarrow\frac{1}{\lambda_{MAB}} \quad\text{as } T\to 0~K. \tag{7.81}$$

## 7.6 Spin Waves

In the ground state at $T=0$ K, all spins in a ferromagnet tend to be aligned parallel ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-10`a). At finite temperature, some spins become misaligned by thermal excitation ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-10`b,c). Including only nearest neighbor interactions, the Heisenberg Hamiltonian becomes

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-82
\mathcal{H}=-2J\sum_{n=1}^{N'}\vec{S}_n\cdot\vec{S}_{n+1}.
```

For the ground state energy ($N$ aligned spins each with spin 1/2):

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-83
E_{G.S.}=-2(N-1)JS^2.
```

To flip one spin gradually produces lower energy increase than flipping abruptly -- a **spin wave** ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-11`).

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-10.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-10
:width: 85%
:align: center
Fig. 7.10: (a) Classical picture of the ground state of a simple ferromagnet; all spins are parallel. (b) A possible excitation; one spin is reversed. (c) The low-lying elementary excitations are spin-waves. The ends of the spin vectors precess on the surfaces of cones, with successive spins advanced in phase by a constant angle.
:::

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-11.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-11
:width: 85%
:align: center
Fig. 7.11: Schematic of a spin wave on a line of spins. (a) The spins viewed in perspective. (b) Spins viewed from above, showing one wavelength. The wave is drawn through the ends of the spin vectors.
:::

For the electron system, dispersion relations take the form

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-84
E(\vec{k})=\frac{\hbar^2 k^2}{2m^*}.
```

A simple derivation yields the spin-wave dispersion relation. At site $p$:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-85
\vec{\mu}_p=g\mu_B\,\vec{S}_p
```

Spin $p$ sees an exchange field:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-86
\vec{H}_p=\frac{2J}{g\mu_B}(\vec{S}_{p-1}+\vec{S}_{p+1}).
```

The torque equation gives:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-88
\frac{d\vec{S}_p}{dt}=\frac{2J}{\hbar}\,\vec{S}_p\times(\vec{S}_{p-1}+\vec{S}_{p+1}).
```

Linearizing (assuming $S_x,S_y\ll S_z$, $S\sim S_z$) yields traveling wave solutions:

$$S_x^p=u e^{i(pka-\omega t)}, \quad S_y^p=v e^{i(pka-\omega t)}, \quad S_z^p={\rm const}. \tag{7.92--7.93}$$

Substitution gives the dispersion relation

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-97
\omega^2=\left[\frac{4JS}{\hbar}(1-\cos ka)\right]^2,
```

so that

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-98
\hbar\omega\simeq 2JSa^2 k^2,
```

valid for $ka\ll 1$.

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-12.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-12
:width: 60%
:align: center
Fig. 7.12: Dispersion relation for spin waves in a one dimensional ferromagnet considering only nearest-neighbor interactions.
:::

Quantization of spin waves gives the **magnon**, with excitation energy

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-99
E_k=n_k\,\hbar\omega_k,
```

where $n_k$ is given by the Bose factor

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-101
n_k=\frac{1}{e^{\hbar\omega_k/k_BT}-1}.
```

The total number of magnons excited at low temperature is proportional to $T^{3/2}$, implying the magnetization near $T=0$ also shows a $T^{3/2}$ dependence.

For an antiferromagnet, the magnon dispersion relation becomes

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-111
\omega_k=\frac{2|J|S}{\hbar}\sin ka \simeq \frac{2|J|S}{\hbar}ka,
```

with finite initial slope at $k=0$, in contrast to ferromagnets where

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-112
\omega_k=\frac{4JS}{\hbar}(1-\cos ka)\simeq\frac{2JS}{\hbar}k^2a^2.
```

Experimentally, however, antiferromagnets also show zero initial slope due to anisotropy field effects.

## 7.7 Anisotropy Energy

The magnetic moments in a magnetic material tend to line up preferentially along certain crystallographic directions. This propensity is expressed in terms of an **anisotropy energy**. For cubic materials the anisotropy energy density $U_K$ is

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-114
U_K = K_1(\alpha_1^2\alpha_2^2+\alpha_2^2\alpha_3^2+\alpha_3^2\alpha_1^2)+K_2\alpha_1^2\alpha_2^2\alpha_3^2+\cdots,
```

where $\alpha_i$ represents direction cosines and $K_1,K_2$ are magnetic anisotropy constants having units of energy density. Only even powers appear due to inversion symmetry and cubic symmetry requirements.

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-16.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-16
:width: 60%
:align: center
Fig. 7.16: Schematic diagram of electronic wave functions showing their anisotropy. Asymmetry of overlap of electron distributions on neighboring ions [difference between spin configurations (a) and (b)] is one mechanism for producing magnetocrystalline anisotropy.
:::

The anisotropy constants are temperature dependent and vanish at the Curie temperature ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-17`).

## 7.8 Magnetic Domains

Macroscopic samples of ferromagnetic materials are not uniformly magnetized, but break up into regions called **domains** ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-18`). The magnetization for each domain has a different orientation. This happens because the magnetic dipole interaction between spins is much weaker than the short-range exchange interaction, but over longer distances forces neighboring regions to have opposite magnetization. The anisotropy energy determines the crystallographic direction within a domain ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-18`).

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-18.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-18
:width: 55%
:align: center
Fig. 7.18: Various possible ferromagnetic domain structures in a single crystal of Fe where the preferred direction of magnetization is along (100). Note that in a magnetic field $H$, the favorably aligned domains grow at the expense of the unfavorable domains.
:::

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-19.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-19
:width: 45%
:align: center
Fig. 7.19: Schematic structure for domains in a sample of finite size. To keep magnetic flux within the sample, the domains near the surface will be magnetized parallel to the surface.
:::

The separation between domains is gradual, occurring over many atomic planes. The transition region is called a **Bloch wall** ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-20`).

:::{figure} images/fig-p3-ch07-20.png
:name: fig-p3-ch07-20
:width: 55%
:align: center
Fig. 7.20: Schematic structure of a Bloch wall separating magnetic domains. In iron the thickness of the transition region is about 300 lattice constants.
:::

From the Heisenberg Hamiltonian for a pair of spins, assuming small angle $\phi$:

$$\mathcal{H}=-2JS^2\cos\phi\simeq-2JS^2+\tfrac{1}{2}JS^2\phi^2=-2JS^2+JS^2\phi^2. \tag{7.116}$$

For $N$ spins in a Bloch wall with total rotation $\pi$ ($\phi=\pi/N$), the exchange energy per unit area is

$$\sigma_W=\sigma_{\rm ex}+\sigma_{\rm anis}=\frac{JS^2\pi^2}{Na^2}+KNa. \tag{7.118--7.119}$$

At equilibrium ($\partial\sigma_W/\partial N=0$):

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-121
N=\sqrt{\frac{JS^2\pi^2}{Ka^3}},
```

which for iron gives $N\sim300$ lattice constants. The equilibrium wall energy:

```{math}
:label: eq-p3-ch07-122
\sigma_W=2\sqrt{\frac{K JS^2\pi^2}{a}},
```

which for iron is $\sim 1$ erg/cm$^2$.

One type of domain particularly important for device applications is the **magnetic bubble** formed in thin films of ferrimagnetic materials (e.g., orthoferrites such as Y$_3$Fe$_5$O$_{12}$). When a magnetic field is applied normal to such a film, domains magnetized oppositely shrink to micron sizes and can be manipulated with in-plane fields.

The **coercive force** $H_c$ is defined as the magnetic field required to reduce the magnetization to zero ({numref}`fig-p3-ch07-21`). Materials with small $H_c$ are **soft magnetic materials** (transformer cores); those with large $H_c$ are **hard magnetic materials** (permanent magnets).
